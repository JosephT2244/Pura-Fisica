Physics

What it is, what it's for, how it's organized into branches and how you become a physicist. Every section adapts to the level you choose.

What is physics?

Physics is the science of «why?» questions. Why does a ball fall? Why does a rainbow have colors? Why does a magnet pull a paper clip? Physicists observe, play with experiments and discover the secret rules that everything follows: from a swing to the stars.

Physics studies matter, energy, space and time, and how they relate. It uses experiments and mathematics to find laws: rules that always work, in your home and in a distant galaxy. For example, the same gravity that makes an apple fall keeps the Moon going around the Earth.

Physics is the natural science that studies the properties of matter and energy and the interactions between them, from subatomic particles to galaxies. Its method combines observation, experiment and mathematical reasoning: models are proposed, quantitative predictions are made and compared with measurements. If a model fails, it is corrected or replaced.

Physics builds mathematical models of natural phenomena from a few principles: conservation of energy, momentum and charge, spacetime symmetries and variational principles. Each theory has a domain of validity: Newtonian mechanics is the limit of relativity when \(v\ll c\) and of quantum mechanics when the typical action is much larger than \(\hbar\).

Today fundamental physics rests on two frameworks: quantum field theory (the Standard Model, with gauge symmetry \(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\)) and general relativity. Both are described by action principles and work with extraordinary precision in their domains, but they have not been unified. Dark matter, dark energy, neutrino masses and the matter–antimatter asymmetry show that the picture is incomplete.

Why does it matter?

Because it helps us understand the world and invent amazing things: airplanes, phones, vaccines kept cold, rockets that reach the Moon. And because it's great fun to wonder how everything works!

  • Understanding the universe. It provides a framework to explain natural phenomena, from rainbows to black holes.
  • Technological development. Most modern technologies come from advances in physics: electricity, telecommunications, electronics, medical imaging.
  • Critical thinking. It teaches you to form hypotheses, measure, estimate errors and decide with evidence: skills useful in any career.
  • The basis of other sciences. Chemistry, biology, geology and engineering rely on physical laws.
  • Global challenges. Clean energy, climate change and new materials all require physics.

Beyond its usefulness, physics is the most successful attempt to describe nature with a minimal number of principles. Its epistemic value lies in its ability to make risky, falsifiable predictions (the electron's anomalous magnetic moment agrees with theory to more than ten significant figures) and in showing where our models fail, which is where new physics appears.

Mechanics

Mechanics studies how things move: why a ball rolls, why you lurch forward when the bus brakes, and why it's harder to push a refrigerator than a shoebox.

Mechanics studies the motion of bodies and the forces that cause it. Isaac Newton summed it up in three laws: objects keep their motion if nothing pushes them (inertia), a force produces an acceleration (F = m·a), and every force has an equal and opposite reaction.

Classical mechanics is divided into kinematics (describes motion with position, velocity and acceleration), dynamics (relates forces and motion through Newton's laws) and statics (equilibrium). Together with the law of universal gravitation, it explains everything from falling bodies to planetary orbits.

Beyond Newton, mechanics is reformulated with the Lagrangian \(L=T-V\) and the principle of least action, which leads to the Euler–Lagrange equations \(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}=0\), and with the Hamiltonian formalism. By Noether's theorem, every continuous symmetry implies a conserved quantity: homogeneity of time → energy; of space → linear momentum; isotropy → angular momentum.

Key discoveries

  • c. 250 BC — Archimedes formulates the principle of the lever and of buoyancy.
  • 1604–1638 — Galileo studies free fall and inclined planes; without air, all bodies fall with the same acceleration.
  • 1609–1619 — Kepler discovers that the planets' orbits are ellipses.
  • 1687 — Newton publishes the Principia: the laws of motion and universal gravitation.
  • 1788–1833 — Lagrange and Hamilton rewrite mechanics in terms of energies instead of forces.

Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics studies heat. Why does chocolate melt in your hand? Why does coffee cool down if you leave it on the table? Heat always goes from hot to cold, never the other way around on its own.

Thermodynamics studies heat, temperature and how thermal energy is turned into work, as in a car engine. Its laws say that energy is conserved and that heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold.

Thermodynamics relates heat, work and internal energy. The first law is energy conservation: \(\Delta U = Q - W\). The second law says that the entropy of an isolated system never decreases, which limits the efficiency of any heat engine. The zeroth law defines temperature, and the third says that absolute zero (0 K = −273.15 °C) is unreachable.

Statistical physics explains thermodynamics in terms of microstates: Boltzmann defined entropy as \(S = k_B\ln\Omega\). Temperature is defined by \(1/T = (\partial S/\partial U)_{V,N}\), and equilibrium properties follow from the partition function \(Z=\sum_i e^{-E_i/k_BT}\).

Key discoveries

  • 1824 — Sadi Carnot analyzes the maximum efficiency of steam engines.
  • 1840s — Joule measures the mechanical equivalent of heat: heat and work are both energy.
  • 1850–1865 — Clausius formulates the second law and introduces entropy.
  • 1877 — Boltzmann relates entropy to the number of microstates.

Electromagnetism

Have you ever rubbed a balloon on your hair and made it stand on end? That's electricity! And the magnets you stick on the fridge are magnetism. The surprising thing is that electricity and magnetism are the same thing… and so is light!

Electromagnetism studies electric charges, currents and magnets. Like charges repel and opposite charges attract; an electric current creates a magnetic field, and a moving magnet can generate current. That's how motors and power plants work.

Electromagnetism describes interactions between charges through electric and magnetic fields. Coulomb's law gives the force between charges; Ampère's law relates currents and magnetic field; Faraday's law says that a changing magnetic flux induces an electromotive force. Maxwell unified everything in four equations and predicted electromagnetic waves, of which light is one.

In differential form, Maxwell's equations are \(\nabla\cdot\vec E=\rho/\varepsilon_0\), \(\nabla\cdot\vec B=0\), \(\nabla\times\vec E=-\partial_t\vec B\) and \(\nabla\times\vec B=\mu_0\vec J+\mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E\). In vacuum they lead to waves with speed \(c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\). Their invariance under Lorentz (not Galilean) transformations was the clue that led to special relativity.

In covariant language, \(\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=\mu_0J^\nu\) and \(\partial_{[\alpha}F_{\beta\gamma]}=0\). Electromagnetism is a \(U(1)\) gauge theory; its quantum version, quantum electrodynamics (QED), is the most precisely tested physical theory.

Key discoveries

  • 1785 — Coulomb measures the force between charges with a torsion balance.
  • 1820 — Ørsted observes that a current deflects a compass.
  • 1831 — Faraday discovers electromagnetic induction.
  • 1865 — Maxwell publishes his theory of the electromagnetic field.
  • 1887 — Hertz produces and detects radio waves.

Optics

Optics studies light. Why can you see yourself in a mirror? Why does a magnifying glass make things big? Why does a straw look «broken» in a glass of water? All of that is light bouncing and bending.

Optics studies how light behaves. When it bounces off a mirror we call it reflection; when it changes direction going from one material to another, refraction. The lenses in glasses, cameras and microscopes use refraction to form images.

Geometrical optics models light as rays: in reflection the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, and in refraction Snell's law holds. Wave optics explains interference, diffraction and polarization, which only make sense if light is an electromagnetic wave.

Geometrical optics is the short-wavelength limit of wave optics (the eikonal approximation) and follows from Fermat's principle: light follows paths of stationary time. Fraunhofer diffraction is the Fourier transform of the aperture. At the quantum level, light is made of photons (quantum optics).

Key discoveries

  • c. 1021 — Ibn al-Haytham writes the Book of Optics: vision is due to light entering the eye.
  • 1621 — Snell finds the law of refraction.
  • 1666 — Newton splits white light with a prism.
  • 1801 — Young shows the interference of light with two slits.
  • 1960 — Maiman builds the first laser.

Modern physics

In the early 1900s scientists discovered that the world of the very small and the very fast follows very strange rules: light comes in tiny packets, time can run slower if you travel very fast, and a particle can be «in two places at once».

Modern physics was born from two 20th-century revolutions: Einstein's relativity, which changed our idea of space and time, and quantum mechanics, which explains how atoms and particles behave. Without them there would be no lasers, computers or GPS.

Special relativity (1905) starts from two postulates: the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames, and light travels at \(c\) for everyone. From these come time dilation, length contraction and \(E=mc^2\). Quantum mechanics (1900–1930) introduces energy quantization, wave–particle duality and the uncertainty principle; its central equation is Schrödinger's.

Special relativity replaces the Galilean group with the Lorentz group and unifies space and time in Minkowski space. General relativity describes gravity as curvature through Einstein's field equations. Quantum mechanics describes states as vectors in a Hilbert space, observables as Hermitian operators, and time evolution through a unitary operator.

Key discoveries

  • 1900 — Planck proposes that energy is emitted in quanta \(E=hf\).
  • 1905 — Einstein: the photoelectric effect, special relativity and \(E=mc^2\).
  • 1913 — Bohr's atomic model.
  • 1915 — General relativity.
  • 1925–1927 — Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Born and Dirac build quantum mechanics.

Origin, evolution and fate: from the Big Bang to us

A very, very long time ago, the whole universe was squeezed together and super hot, and it began to stretch. Over time, stars and planets formed… and on one of them, the Earth, life appeared and, much later, us. We are made of stardust!

Cosmology studies the history of the universe. About 13.8 billion years ago the expansion we call the Big Bang began. The atoms in your body (like carbon and iron) were made inside stars that exploded before the Sun was born. Physics tells us where we come from and what future the universe might have.

The standard cosmological model (ΛCDM) describes a universe expanding from a hot, dense state. The main evidence is Hubble expansion, the cosmic microwave background (discovered in 1965) and the abundance of light elements. Today the universe is made of about 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter and 68% dark energy, which accelerates the expansion.

The dynamics of the expansion follow from the Friedmann equations, \(H^2=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho-\frac{kc^2}{a^2}+\frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}\), with \(H=\dot a/a\). Each component dilutes its density differently (\(\rho_m\propto a^{-3}\), \(\rho_r\propto a^{-4}\), \(\rho_\Lambda\) constant), which defines eras dominated by radiation, matter and dark energy.

Open tensions remain: the value of \(H_0\) measured locally (~73 km/s/Mpc) does not match the one inferred from the background radiation (~67 km/s/Mpc), and recent galaxy-survey results suggest dark energy might evolve. The nature of dark matter and the mechanism of inflation are still not established.

Being a physicist

Why study physics?

  • Curiosity. You work on the deepest questions: what everything is made of, how the universe began, what time is.
  • Versatility. People who study physics learn to model, program, measure and solve hard problems; that's why they work in research, industry, finance, data science, medicine, energy and education.
  • Impact. Clean energy, medical diagnosis, quantum computing, new materials: physics is behind the big challenges.
  • Transferable skills. Mathematics, programming, data analysis and critical thinking.
