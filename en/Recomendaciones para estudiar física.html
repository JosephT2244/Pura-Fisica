How to study physics

A method that works, a checklist for solving any problem, the complete path of what to learn and the best resources for each level. It includes a timer for studying in blocks.

A method that works

To learn physics, play and ask! Do simple experiments at home (with balls, magnets, flashlights), ask «why?» and look for the answer with an adult. Draw what you observe and tell someone about it: if you can explain it, you understood it.

  1. First the idea, then the formula. Before memorizing \(F=ma\), understand what it means: more force, more acceleration; more mass, less acceleration.
  2. Do problems, lots of them. Physics is learned with a pencil in hand. Reading solutions isn't the same as solving.
  3. Active recall. Close your notebook and write down what you remember; then compare. It's much more effective than rereading.
  4. Spaced review. Review a topic the next day, after a week and after a month. That way it stays in long-term memory.
  5. The Feynman technique. Explain the topic in simple words, as if to a child. Wherever you get stuck is what you don't understand.
  6. Check units and orders of magnitude. If a car comes out at 3,000 m/s, something is wrong.
  7. Sleep. Sleep consolidates what you learned; staying up before an exam pays off less than sleeping well.

On top of that: master the mathematical tools at the same time as the physics (vector calculus, differential equations, linear algebra); derive formulas instead of memorizing them; solve problems from several books, because each author trains different intuitions; learn to program simple simulations (Python with NumPy and Matplotlib) and form study groups where each person explains a problem at the board.

In graduate school and research, the challenge is no longer solving problems but posing them. Read original papers (arXiv), reproduce classic results with your own calculations, keep a research notebook, discuss your ideas early with others and practice writing: a result that isn't communicated well barely exists.

Experiment with your phone

Your phone has an accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer, microphone and light sensor: it's a pocket lab! The free app phyphox (from RWTH Aachen University) lets you measure an elevator's acceleration, a pendulum's period, the frequency of your voice or a magnet's field. And with Tracker you can analyze videos of motion frame by frame.

Common mistakes when studying physics

  • Memorizing formulas without understanding what each letter means.
  • Plugging in numbers from the start instead of working with symbols.
  • Forgetting units or mixing systems (km/h with seconds).
  • Not drawing: a good diagram solves half the problem.
  • Reading the solution before trying the problem for at least 15 minutes.
  • Studying everything the night before instead of a little every day.

Olympiads and competitions

If you like physics, olympiads are the best way to take it further. In Mexico, the National Physics Olympiad organized by the Mexican Physical Society selects the students who represent the country at the Ibero-American Olympiad and the International Physics Olympiad. Problems from past olympiads are excellent practice material.
