Stop 1 · your hand

It all starts with a question

Why does an apple fall? Galileo showed that, without air, everything falls with the same acceleration, and Newton realized that the same force pulling the apple keeps the Moon in orbit. Look at the dots: they mark where the ball is at equal time intervals. They get farther apart each time: it is speeding up.

Stop 2 · light

White light hides a rainbow

Newton passed sunlight through a prism and split it into colors. Each color is a wave with a different wavelength, from about 400 to 700 nanometers. And visible light is only a thin slice of a spectrum that runs from radio waves to gamma rays.

Stop 3 · the atom

Inside everything there are atoms

If you blew an apple up to the size of the Earth, its atoms would be about the size of marbles. Electrons don't orbit like planets: they form clouds of probability, and when they jump from one level to another they give off light of an exact color. That is how we know what stars are made of.

Stop 4 · the nucleus

Deeper still: quarks and gluons

The nucleus is tens of thousands of times smaller than the atom, yet it holds almost all of its mass. Each proton has three quarks bound by gluons, with a force that does not weaken as you pull them apart: that's why we never see a lone quark. Almost all of your mass comes from the energy of that bond.

Explore particles
Stop 5 · spacetime

Mass bends space and time

Einstein discovered that gravity is not a mysterious pull: the Sun bends spacetime and the Earth follows the curve. Near a mass, time runs slower; your GPS corrects for it every day.

Explore relativity
Stop 6 · the universe

Space itself is stretching

Distant galaxies move away from each other because the space between them grows, and it has been doing so for about 13.8 billion years. The matter that stars, planets and you are made of is just 5% of the universe: the rest is dark matter and dark energy, two of today's great mysteries.

Why doesn't the Moon fall onto the Earth?

It is falling! But it moves sideways so fast that, as it falls, the Earth «curves away» beneath it. It's like swinging a ball tied to a string: the string is gravity.

Earth's gravity pulls on the Moon all the time. Because the Moon also moves sideways at about 1 km/s, instead of crashing it follows a curve around us: an orbit. Newton realized it is the same force that makes an apple fall.

The Moon is in free fall: the gravitational pull \(F=GMm/r^2\) acts as the centripetal force, \(GMm/r^2=mv^2/r\). With \(r\approx384\,000\) km you get \(v\approx1.02\) km/s and a period of about 27.3 days, just what is observed.

It's a two-body problem with potential \(-GMm/r\): the energy \(E=\tfrac12\mu\dot r^2+\frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}-\frac{GMm}{r}\) is negative, so the orbit is bound (an ellipse with eccentricity ≈ 0.055). Conserved angular momentum keeps \(r\) from going to 0.

In general relativity the Moon follows a geodesic of the spacetime curved by the Earth; corrections to Newton are of order \(GM/rc^2\sim10^{-11}\), but lunar laser ranging measures them (geodetic precession ≈ 19 mas/yr). Tides also transfer angular momentum: the Moon recedes about 3.8 cm per year.
